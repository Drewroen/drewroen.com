# Is America sue-happy?

Almost everyone has heard the claim: we live in a sue-happy country, where people drag each other into court over spilled coffee and hurt feelings, and juries hand out lottery tickets. It is a durable piece of folk economics. It is also, as a description of how often Americans actually sue, mostly wrong — and wrong in a specific way that's worth naming, because the thing that has genuinely changed is something else entirely.

Short version: filings are flat to falling, personal-injury claims are a small and shrinking slice of the docket, and fewer than 4 in 100 civil cases get anywhere near a trial. What has grown is a mass docket of debt-collection and eviction cases, where the plaintiff is almost never a private individual, and the size of the very largest corporate verdicts.

## What state courts actually process

State courts fielded an estimated 13.7 million civil filings in 2022, about 21% of everything state courts handle (National Center for State Courts). Open that pile up and the petty-squabble story gets thin:

- **Contract cases were 37%** of the civil caseload in 2022, down from 44% in 2018 — and these are mostly debt collection, landlord-tenant, and foreclosure.
- **Small claims were 19%.**
- **Tort** — personal injury, malpractice, defamation, the bin that "sue-happy" arguments are really about — **was about 6% of civil filings in 2024**, per the NCSC's caseload dashboards, and has sat in the mid single digits for every year in the series.
- Probate/estate, real property, and miscellaneous civil made up the rest.

The best case-level study of the question is NCSC's *Landscape of Civil Litigation in State Courts*, which read every non-domestic civil case disposed in 152 courts across ten urban counties in 2012–13: 925,344 cases, about 5% of the national civil caseload. Nearly two-thirds were contract cases, and more than half of those were debt collection and landlord-tenant. Small claims were 16%, tort 7%, real property 1%.

Its money findings are what really kill the anecdote. The median judgment across all those cases was **$2,441**; three-quarters were under about $5,200. Only 4% of cases were disposed by trial, summary judgment, or binding arbitration. At least one side went without a lawyer in more than three-quarters of cases — usually the defendant, who frequently never appeared at all.

That is not a picture of a country litigating its grievances. It's a picture of a collections system with a courthouse attached.

## The trend line runs down

- Tort filings in state courts **fell 13% between 2018 and 2022**, across the 31 states with comparable data (NCSC).
- In the nation's 75 largest counties, civil trials **fell by more than half between 1992 and 2005**; tort trials fell 31.5% between 1996 and 2005 (Bureau of Justice Statistics).
- Trials accounted for about **3.4% of general civil dispositions** in 2005 (BJS). The vanishing trial has been a forty-year trend, not a recent wobble.
- Tort was roughly half the civil caseload in BJS's 1992 survey of large counties and 7% in the 2013 *Landscape* study. Part of that gap is definitional — the later study includes limited-jurisdiction courts, which are wall-to-wall collection cases — but no reading of the two numbers makes the direction go up.

Most injured people never sue at all. RAND's national survey of accident victims found that only **19% even considered** pursuing a liability claim; the figure usually quoted from the fuller report is that about 10% made some kind of claim, including a demand letter to an insurer, and about **2% filed a lawsuit**. That survey is from 1991, so treat it as an order of magnitude rather than a 2026 reading — but nothing since has produced a number pointing the other way, and RAND's central finding (that the tort system is *under*-used relative to the injuries it covers) has held up.

Nor is any of this new. The historian Wayne McIntosh, counting complaints in St. Louis, found **more civil filings per capita in the 1820s, '30s and '40s than in the 1960s and '70s** — the comparison Marc Galanter used in *Reading the Landscape of Disputes* (1983) to argue that the "litigation explosion" was largely a press phenomenon. Cross-country comparisons are too patchy to settle whether the US sues more than, say, Germany, whatever you've read.

One more piece of evidence that filings track law rather than temperament: when states tighten tort rules, filings fall within a year or two, and when they loosen them, filings rise. Litigiousness that moves with statute is not a culture.

## What is actually rising

Two things, and neither is a nation of petty litigants.

**Debt collection.** In the roughly 17–20 jurisdictions reporting early data, contract filings went from about 762,000 in 2021 to more than 1.3 million in 2025, and small claims from about 671,000 to nearly 1 million, while tort and real property stayed flat (NCSC, August 2026). Note the caveat: that's a reporting panel, not a national total. This is the one place where litigation volume is genuinely climbing, and the plaintiff is a collector, a landlord, or a hospital billing office filing by the thousand. It's a filing-cost and default-judgment story far more than a litigiousness story.

**The top of the distribution.** Verdicts of $10 million or more against corporate defendants rose from 135 in 2024 to about 190 in 2025 — up 40.7% — totaling more than $25.6 billion, with 40-plus over $100 million and four over $1 billion (Marathon Strategies). Caveats, and they matter: Marathon is a crisis-communications firm whose clients are corporate defendants; it counts reported verdicts rather than final payouts, and big awards often get reduced, reversed, or settled quietly after trial. But a 41% jump in one year, on top of a similar jump the year before, is too large to be an artifact of counting.

So the defensible version of "sue happy" is narrower than the slogan: **the biggest cases have become much bigger, while the number of cases has not.** That's a story about damages, insurance, and litigation finance — a severity trend, not a frequency trend.

Worth separating from both: there is real money in legal advertising, billions a year by industry estimates. The billboards are genuine, and they are marketing, not filing data.

## Why the belief survives

Three reasons, none of them stupid.

The anecdotes are real. Somewhere today a jury awarded someone an absurd sum, and it made the news because it was absurd. Availability does the rest: nobody writes a story about the 96% of civil cases that ended in a settlement, a dismissal, or a default judgment.

The cases that go to trial are, by construction, the outliers. The ordinary case settles or defaults. The ~3% that don't are the ones where both sides think they can win — which is exactly the sample that produces strange verdicts, and exactly the sample that any conversation about "juries these days" draws on.

And the phrase has a sponsor. "Frivolous lawsuit" and "sue-happy nation" were popularized by a corporate tort-reform campaign that dates to the 1970s and '80s, documented at the time by Galanter and others; the plaintiff's bar runs its own messaging in the other direction. Both are selling something. The filing statistics are not.

## Verdict

If "sue happy" means ordinary people hauling each other into court over trivia: **mostly false.** Filings per capita are flat to down, tort is a mid single-digit slice of the civil docket, trials are vanishing, and most injured people never claim at all. The median civil judgment in the largest case-level study was under $2,500.

If it means the legal system is expensive, slow, and capable of producing enormous awards against companies: **true**, and the largest verdicts genuinely are getting larger. But that's a claim about damages and risk, not about Americans being quarrelsome — and it describes the behavior of insurers, courts, and litigators far more than it describes the people supposedly filing suit over nothing.
